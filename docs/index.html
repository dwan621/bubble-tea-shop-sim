1.What the four layers add to the course model

The original course model asked two questions: should a bubble-tea shop triage fast items ahead of the tea queue, and should it hand the ice cream of a mixed order over as soon as it is ready? It answered them with one clerk pool, stationary Poisson arrivals, fixed service times and a 2×2 policy matrix. Version 2 keeps that engine as the special case it always was and adds four optional layers around it: an intra-day demand profile, a station network with skills and shifts, an inventory bill of materials with shelf life, and an economics layer with pricing, SLA and a fairness cap.

baseline keptsame engineall numbers regeneratedAnyLogic archive, not rerunnable

The engine is an extension, not a replacement

With every layer switched off, the engine reproduces the original model exactly. The build-time check compares baseline_flat against scenarios.mixed_order_baseline(1.25) at seed 7 and the two metric dictionaries are identical. That is the contract: the four-layer model adds mechanisms, it does not change the old answers.

The archived AnyLogic model (archive/anylogic/, screenshots in docs/assets/slides/) produced the original E1–E6 numbers. Architecture

Each layer is a frozen specification object (data_spec.py) that the loader turns into a SimConfig; scenarios are JSON files composing the layers they need. The engine dispatches events per station and staff skill when a network is present, and keeps the baseline dispatch rule when the configuration is baseline-compatible.

2.The scenario library

The layer study runs on a library of shop contexts instead of a single configuration. Each file in data/scenarios/ composes the layers it needs and carries a provenance block that names its source, its method and its uncertainty. No scenario claims field data: the library is a set of synthetic teaching cases anchored on the course assumptions, and the uncertainty field says so.

The catalogue below uses 100 replicates per scenario (seed 0, stride 1), the same convention as the layer studies, and is generated by analysis/run_demand_network.py. It is a map of contexts, not a causal comparison: every row changes several things at once (crew, peaks, stations, stock), so read it as a spread, not as an experiment. The layer sections that follow isolate one mechanism at a time.

3.Design and what the builder validates

The experimental design is the baseline design, generalised: one simulated day (480 minutes, 60-minute warm-up excluded from service metrics), fixed seeds, paired comparisons on common random numbers, and a reported 95% confidence interval for every mean. The layer studies keep the same seed convention (seed 0, stride 1) so that a table cell can be regenerated on its own.

Read the catalogue as a map, not as an experiment

The scenario catalogue runs 100 replicates per context, like the layer studies, but its rows change several things at once: crew size, peak shape, stations, stock. It is a map of contexts, not a controlled experiment; causal claims come only from the layer sections, which vary one mechanism at a time on common random numbers.

4.Layer 1: the cost of a peak

This is the experiment the course model could not run. The original model had a flat arrival rate, so a busy hour and a busy day were the same thing. The demand layer separates them: the peak and the flat profile below have the same daily mean demand, and differ only in shape.

The peak profile (peak-to-mean ×1.49) raises the peak-hour busy ratio from 66.0% to 77.7%, multiplies the p95 wait by 1.46× and lifts the walk-away rate from 6.9% to 15.1% — while the daily utilisation actually falls from 60.6% to 54.9%, because abandoning customers remove work from the system. Profit per hour drops from 296.0 to 239.3.

What the peak does to the shop

Decision rule from this section: plan for the peak, not for the average. A shop that staffs to its daily utilisation will be fine on paper and short at lunch. The next layer asks what happens when the staff are organised into stations rather than one pool.

5.Layer 1: variability, patience and group orders

Peaks are not the only way demand stops being a smooth rate. The shop also sees variable make times, customers with finite patience, and group arrivals. These mechanisms are often discussed separately; the engine can switch each on while holding the others fixed.

Service-time variability: the tail, not the mean

Replacing deterministic make times by exponential ones — the engine forces exponential sampling, i.e. CV = 1, whenever the variability kind is exponential — moves the p95 by 4.62 minutes at λ = 1.0 and 6.71 minutes at λ = 1.2, while the mean time in system moves by only 2.21 minutes. The p95 ratio at lognormal CV 0.75 versus deterministic is 1.25× at λ = 1.2: the tail grows with load much faster than the mean.

Patience: who leaves, and why

With balking active at arrival, reneging almost never binds (reneging_binds_in_grid = True): the customer who would have reneged has usually already walked away at arrival. At λ = 1.2 the walk-away rate falls from 29.3% with a 3-minute threshold to 3.2% with a 12-minute one. Patience is therefore a demand-side lever: it decides how much of the queue becomes lost sales.

Group orders: burstiness versus demand

Batches are easy to misread. Holding the arrival-event rate fixed, a 30% batch probability raises the walk-away rate from 7.5% to 22.7% — but that is mostly a demand increase, because each event brings several customers. Holding the daily customer mean fixed, the same probability leaves the walk-away rate at 9.3% and profit at 219.8/h versus 235.4/h for singles. Burstiness is real but second-order next to the peak shape.

6.Layer 2: the bottleneck moves

The course model had one homogeneous clerk pool, so the only possible bottleneck was the pool itself. The network layer splits the work into stations — order counter, tea station, ice machine, bread counter — with staff that have skills, and asks a different question: when the shop adds capacity, where does the constraint move to?

The study sweeps tea-station capacity 1–4 with baristas synced to stations, at λ = 0.6, 0.9 and 1.2. Critical capacity — the smallest capacity whose extra station no longer pays for itself on paired common random numbers — is lambda 0.6: 3, lambda 0.9: 3, lambda 1.2: 4. The tea station remains the busiest station at every capacity, but beyond the critical capacity the queue is decided by the shared barista pool and the order counter, not by the tea station.

The bottleneck migrates

The bottleneck is not a property of the shop — it is a property of the shop plus its staff plan. Adding tea stations helps until the barista pool or the order counter becomes the binding resource; after that, more equipment only moves the queue. This is the classic capacity-planning insight, but here it is measured with paired confidence intervals rather than asserted.

Skills: the pooling gain lives in the generalist

With the same headcount and the same wage bill, turning four dedicated staff into four generalists is worth +84.54 ± 5.13/h. One-way cross-training — a barista who can also run the register — is worth +0.17 ± 0.22/h, i.e. nothing measurable: the flexibility has to go both ways to matter.

Shifts and breaks: removing capacity near the peak

The shift experiment adds realistic staffing: staggered unpaid breaks, or a mid-shift barista. The peak-hour mean LOS is 5.19 min with no breaks, 7.37 min with a staggered break and 5.24 min with the mid-shift barista. Breaks remove capacity exactly when demand is highest, so both the peak-hour wait and the p95 jump while the wage bill falls — a net loss. The mid-shift barista adds headcount but leaves the peak-hour wait essentially unchanged (+0.06 ± 0.08 min): at the peak the tea station, not headcount, is the binding resource, so the extra wage line buys little.

8.Layer 3: fill rate, waste and profit

Inventory study caveats (as recorded in its metadata)

  • fill_rate divides by all arrivals (including the warm-up hour); the money and served metrics are post-warm-up. Morning stock-outs therefore appear in fill_rate but not in served.
  • purchase_cost charges every purchased unit including stock left at closing and re-charges the opening stock on every replicate, so it must be read next to fill_rate / stockout_losses.
  • waste_units is a lower bound: expiry is evaluated lazily when an ingredient is next touched.
  • A single outstanding order per ingredient (the engine's (s, S) rule), so an order quantity below the lead-time demand guarantees a stock-out.

9.Layer 4: peak pricing and elasticity

The pricing layer charges a multiplier inside the two campus peaks (minutes 60–180 and 300–420) and lets demand react with a constant elasticity: a multiplier m scales arrivals by m^ε. The question is not whether peak pricing raises revenue — it usually does — but what it does to the tail, and whether the profit maximum is a policy recommendation or an artefact of the cost model.

Reading the optima. Revenue and profit both rise with the multiplier at every tested elasticity, but the mechanism is demand shedding: fewer, better-paying orders inside the peak also mean less waiting. The useful decision variable is the service-neutral multiplier: the largest increase that leaves the tail essentially unchanged. Anything above it buys profit by lowering service, and the model prices that trade only through the waiting cost of customers who stay — not through the ones who balk. That is a modelling limit, not a discovery about pricing.

Pricing study caveats (as recorded in its metadata)

  • profit_per_hour subtracts the waiting cost of the served customers only, so demand shedding (higher price, fewer arrivals) looks profitable twice: fewer lost sales and less waiting cost. The profit-maximising multiplier therefore sits at the edge of the tested range and must not be read as a policy recommendation on its own.
  • Compensation is a pure transfer: it never changes service times, so los_p95 is identical across the SLA grid. Only capacity/pricing changes attainment.
  • campus_rush has no inventory layer (its scenario file carries no BOM), so the good-manager scenario covers pricing + SLA + fairness + network. Cross-layer inventory coupling is out of scope for this study.
  • The fairness experiment keeps campus_rush's demand profile, classes and patience, but drops the network layer and sets workers=3 so the queue is visible to the starvation cap; that is the split+fast high-load cell.

10.Layer 4: SLA compensation and fairness

The SLA layer turns lateness into money: a customer whose time in system exceeds the target costs the shop a fixed compensation. The fairness layer addresses the queueing side of the same problem: with fast-item priority a tea order can wait behind a stream of fast jobs, so an ageing cap escalates any job that has waited too long.

11.The management bundle

Each layer study isolates one mechanism. What the bundle implies for practice

  • Buy capacity before you buy cleverness. The capacity lever dominates the ranking; policy tweaks are second-order against a fourth barista at the peak.
  • Protect the tail, not the average. The fairness cap and the service-neutral price multiplier both act on p95 at little cost, which is where customers actually leave.
  • Price to shed the peak, not to raise the average ticket. The profitable pricing window is the one that moves demand out of the peak windows without worsening the tail.
  • Keep the SLA explicit. Compensation does not improve service; it prices the miss. That is what makes the capacity decision a business decision rather than a queueing preference.

Continuity and revision

What did not change: the engine, the 2×2 policy matrix and the direction of the headline baseline result. Fast-item priority is still the stronger lever than split delivery at the baseline operating point, and split delivery still stops paying as load rises.

What validation does not prove

These checks rule out silent regressions; they do not validate the model against a real shop. Treat the layer studies as a disciplined way to compare mechanisms, not as calibrated forecasts.
